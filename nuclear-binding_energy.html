A nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons it is made of. The difference is the mass defect, `Delta m = Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n - m_(nuc)`, and the energy equivalent of that mass is the binding energy, `E_b = (Delta m) c^2`: the energy you must supply to pull the nucleus completely apart, and the energy released when it forms. With 1 u = 931.5 MeV/`c^2`, a mass defect in atomic mass units converts directly into MeV. (With atomic masses, use the mass of the hydrogen-1 atom for each proton so that the electrons cancel.)

The binding energy per nucleon, `BEN = E_b/A`, is the average energy needed to remove one nucleon. Plotted against `A` for stable nuclei, it rises steeply for light nuclei (with a spike at helium-4), reaches about 8.8 MeV near iron-56, and falls slowly for heavy nuclei. Nuclei near the top are the most tightly bound. Fusing light nuclei, or splitting heavy ones, moves toward the top and releases energy.

The energy to remove one particular nucleon, the separation energy, is the difference of two binding energies, for example `S_n = E_b(Z, A) - E_b(Z, A - 1)`. Typical nuclear binding energies are a million times larger than the 13.6 eV that holds the electron in a hydrogen atom.
