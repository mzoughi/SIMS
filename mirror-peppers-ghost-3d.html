How to make the video for the phone pyramid

  1. Black background. Film or render the subject against pure black. Black sends no light to the panels, so it reflects nothing and the subject seems to float.
  2. Four views if you can. Record the subject from the front, right, back and left (turn it 90° between takes, or use four cameras in 3D software). One view copied four times also works, but then every viewer sees the same side.
  3. Square layout. Use a square canvas, as tall as the screen is wide. Leave an empty black square in the centre, the size of the small end of the pyramid.
  4. Feet toward the centre. Put one view on each side of the centre square, with the feet toward the centre and the head toward the edge. The front view goes on the side nearest the front viewer.
  5. Flip, don't just rotate. Each panel is a mirror and reverses the picture. Mirror each view (flip it) before placing it. With rotated copies only, text and right-hand/left-hand details come out backwards: try it with the P.
  6. Play it right. Keep the four views in step, export, and play full screen at maximum brightness in a darkened room.

Pepper's ghost is not a hologram. A clear pane lets you see the scene behind it and, at the same time, reflects a little light from a brightly lit object hidden from direct view. Your eye adds the two: the reflection, a virtual image, seems to stand among the real objects behind the pane. Drag the scene, or use the arrow keys, to look at it from any side.

1. Mirror and glass. You look straight at the pane, along its normal. A letter P stands in front of it; its front is orange and its back is blue. A plane mirror forms a virtual image as far behind the pane as the object is in front of it,

`d_i = d_o`,   `m = h_i/h_o = +1`.

The image is upright and the same size, but reversed front to back: the pane shows the side of the P that faces it. A ray that hits the pane along the normal comes straight back. A silvered mirror reflects about 90% of the light and hides everything behind it. Clear glass, at normal incidence, reflects only

`R = ((n-1)/(n+1))^2 ~~ 0.04`

at each surface (`n ~~ 1.5`), about 8% in all, and transmits the rest, `T = 1 - R`. The image forms in exactly the same place, only fainter, and the plant behind the glass shows through. How visible the image is depends on the light: a simple measure is the share of the light reaching the eye along that direction that comes from the reflection,

`V = (R I_(obj))/(R I_(obj) + T I_(behind))`.

A bright object and a dark space behind the glass make a convincing ghost.

2. The theatre ghost. A large sheet of glass stands between the audience and the stage, turned at about 45°. Beside the stage, out of the audience's sight, is a dark booth. When a lamp lights the actor in the booth, the glass reflects a faint image of the actor, and the audience sees a ghost on the stage, as far behind the glass as the actor is in front of it. Turning the lamp off makes the ghost vanish; moving the actor moves the ghost. The ghost is a mirror image: the lantern held in the actor's right hand is in the ghost's left. In the original 19th-century version the hidden room was below the front of the stage and the glass leaned toward the audience; the optics are the same.

3. The phone pyramid. Four clear plastic trapezoids form an upside-down, truncated pyramid on a phone screen. Each panel, tilted at `alpha` to the screen, is a plane mirror for the picture next to it. The image of the flat picture is turned through `2 alpha`; at 45° it stands upright, floating in the middle. Each viewer sees the image formed by the panel facing them. It is four flat reflections, not a three-dimensional image.

4. Making the video. Four views of the subject are arranged around an empty black centre, feet toward the centre. Because each panel reverses its picture, each view must be flipped (mirrored), not only rotated. With four camera views, walking around the pyramid shows the subject from different sides.

The scene is drawn in 3D with flat shaded polygons. Reflected images are computed by reflecting every point of the object across the plane of the pane or panel. The visibility measure is a simple model that ignores absorption, multiple reflections and the response of the eye.
