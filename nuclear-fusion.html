Nuclear Fusion — Joining Light Nuclei

In fusion, two light nuclei combine into a heavier one. The product is lighter than the reactants, so energy is released, Q = (Δm)c². But both nuclei are positive: they repel, and must get within a few femtometres of each other (the Coulomb barrier) before the attractive strong force can take over. That takes temperatures of millions of kelvin, and even then the nuclei get through only by quantum tunneling.

Nucleosynthesis is the making of the elements. Hydrogen and most helium come from the Big Bang. Everything else up to iron was made by fusion inside stars, each stage burning the “ash” of the previous one at a higher temperature. Fusion stops at iron, the top of the binding-energy curve; heavier elements are made by neutron capture, mostly in supernovae and neutron-star mergers.

The hydrogen bomb (thermonuclear weapon) uses an uncontrolled fusion reaction. Fusion needs extreme temperature and pressure; in the bomb these are produced by a fission explosion. The fusion fuel is usually solid lithium-6 deuteride: neutrons turn lithium into tritium, which fuses with the deuterium. Unlike a fission bomb, there is no critical mass for the fusion fuel, so the yield can be made far larger. Schematic only.

1 kiloton of TNT = 4.184×1012 J. The first thermonuclear test (Ivy Mike, 1952) yielded about 10 megatons, roughly 700 times the Hiroshima fission bomb. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty ended atmospheric testing by its signatories; the effects of these weapons, blast, heat, radiation and fallout, are why their control remains a central question.

Nuclear fusion is the process in which light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus. The product nucleus lies higher on the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve than the reactants, so mass is lost and energy released: `Q = (Delta m) c^2`. For example `\ _1^2 H + \ _1^3 H -> \ _2^4 He + \ _0^1 n + 17.6` MeV. Because both nuclei are positive, they must be brought close enough (a few fm) for the strong force to act, against the Coulomb barrier `U = k q_1 q_2 /r`. This requires temperatures of millions of kelvin, and even then the nuclei usually get through by quantum tunneling.

The Sun is powered by the proton–proton chain, whose net effect is `4\ _1^1 H -> \ _2^4 He + 2e^+ + 2 nu` with a release of 26.7 MeV. Nucleosynthesis: hydrogen and helium come from the Big Bang; heavier elements up to iron are made by fusion in stars, in successive burning stages; elements beyond iron are made by neutron capture in supernovae and neutron-star mergers.

A hydrogen bomb uses an uncontrolled fusion reaction ignited by a fission explosion; it has no critical-mass limit, so its yield can be very large (the fourth tab is a schematic only). A fusion reactor must heat a deuterium–tritium plasma to about `1.5 xx 10^8` K and confine it. The power-flow tab follows the energy: fusion power `P_(fus) = Q_(sci) P_(heat)`, part of the electricity produced is recirculated to heat the plasma, and the plant is useful only if the net electricity is positive. Per kilogram, D–T fusion releases about four times as much energy as U-235 fission. Masses are rounded atomic masses; tunneling probabilities use the Gamow factor; historical data are approximate.
