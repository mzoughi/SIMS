A Pentatonic Pan Flute

Pipe 1 (C5)
Blow across the top of a pipe like a bottle. Each pipe is capped at the bottom, so its air column holds a quarter wavelength: f = v/(4L). You can also click a pipe to select it.
How were the pipe lengths chosen?

Each pipe is cut to one note. A capped pipe resonates when its length is a quarter of the wavelength, so L = v/(4f) with v ≈ 343 m/s. Higher notes need shorter pipes. That is why a pan flute looks like a staircase.

The scale. The pipes play a C major pentatonic scale over two octaves: C D E G A, then C D E G A again, one octave higher. A pentatonic scale leaves out two notes of the ordinary major scale (F and B), which is why almost any sequence of its notes sounds pleasant together. Many folk traditions use it.

Octaves. Going up one octave doubles the frequency, so the pipe must be half as long. Pipes 6–10 are exactly half the length of pipes 1–5.

Uncapping the bottoms. An open–open pipe holds half a wavelength, f = v/(2L), so every pipe jumps up an octave. As explained for the single straw, a real open pipe is much harder to sound by blowing across it; the simulation plays the ideal tone anyway.

A caveat. Real pipes are cut a little shorter than v/(4f), because the air vibrating just above the open top acts like extra length (the end correction, about 0.6 × the pipe radius).

A pan flute is a set of pipes, each cut to play one note. Blowing across the top of a pipe, like across a bottle, makes the air column inside it resonate. A pipe capped at the bottom holds a quarter of a wavelength, so

`f = v/(4L)`,   `L = v/(4f)`,

with `v ~~ 343` m/s: higher notes need shorter pipes, which is why a pan flute looks like a staircase. The pipes play a C major pentatonic scale over two octaves. Uncapping the bottoms makes open pipes, which hold half a wavelength (`f = v/(2L)`) and sound an octave higher. The air displacement and pressure in the chosen pipe can be shown.
