A person sits on a stool that turns freely about a vertical axis, holding a bicycle wheel by its axle. The wheel spins fast: its angular momentum `vec L_w = I_w omega_w` points along its axle (by the right-hand rule).

The stool can turn only about the vertical, and nothing outside exerts a torque about that axis, so the vertical component of the total angular momentum stays constant. (The horizontal components are not conserved: the floor and the stool's bearing can push on them.) With the axle tilted by `phi` from the vertical, the wheel's vertical component is `I_w omega_w cos phi`, so

`I_w omega_w cos phi + I_p Omega = "constant"`,

where `I_p` and `Omega` are the moment of inertia and the angular speed of the person and stool. Starting at rest with the axle sideways (no vertical component), turning the axle to point up makes the person turn the opposite way. Starting with the axle up and flipping it to point down changes the wheel's vertical component from `+L_w` to `-L_w`: the person must take `2 L_w`, and turns at `Omega = 2 I_w omega_w / I_p`. To tilt the axle, the person's hands exert a torque on the wheel; the wheel exerts the opposite torque on the person, which sets them turning.
