How a Geiger Counter Works

A Geiger–Müller tube is a metal cylinder (the cathode, at 0 V) filled with a gas at low pressure, with a thin wire along its axis (the anode, at a high positive voltage). Radiation that enters ionizes a few gas atoms; the freed electrons rush to the wire, where the electric field is so strong that each one sets off an avalanche of new ions. The avalanche spreads along the whole wire, and the tube gives one large electrical pulse: one click. Send a particle, then change the voltage.

Reading the meter

A Geiger–Müller (GM) tube is a gas-filled metal cylinder (the cathode) with a thin wire on its axis (the anode) held at a high positive voltage `V`. Between a wire of radius `a` and a cylinder of radius `b` the electric field points radially and grows sharply near the wire:

`E(r) = V/(r ln(b//a))`.

An ionizing particle frees a few electrons in the gas. They drift toward the wire, and within a few wire radii of it the field is strong enough for each electron to gain the ionization energy between collisions: it frees another electron, both accelerate and free more, and an avalanche forms. At a low voltage the number of ions collected equals the number made by the particle (ionization chamber); at higher voltages it is multiplied by a fixed factor (proportional counter). In the Geiger region, ultraviolet photons from each avalanche start new avalanches all along the wire, and the discharge stops only when the slow sheath of positive ions around the wire weakens the field. Every particle, whatever its type or energy, then gives the same large pulse, about `10^9` to `10^10` ions: a Geiger counter counts particles but cannot tell their energy.

The positive ions take about 100 µs to drift to the wall; during this dead time the tube cannot register a new particle. A quench gas (a halogen or an organic vapour) takes the energy of the ions reaching the wall, so they do not free new electrons and restart the discharge. Above the plateau the tube discharges continuously. Over the Geiger plateau the count rate hardly depends on the voltage, which is where a tube is operated.

A survey meter shows the count rate in counts per minute. With a dead time `tau`, a true rate `n` is measured as `m = n/(1 + n tau)`, so a high true rate can be recovered from `n = m/(1 - m tau)`. Counts are random, so a count `N` has an uncertainty of about `sqrt(N)`. The ion numbers, voltages, sample count rates and the dose conversion (about 330 CPM per µSv/h for Cs-137) are typical, approximate values; the animation is slowed by factors of a million or more.
