A radioactive source sends radiation toward a Geiger–Müller counter. Each time an ionizing particle or photon enters its tube, the gas inside is ionized, a pulse of current flows and the counter clicks. Turn the sound on to hear it. Because decays are random, the clicks are irregular and the count in each second varies; the 10-second count gives a steadier value (its typical random error is about `sqrt(N)`).

Put absorbers between the source and the counter: alpha particles are stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air; beta particles pass paper but are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium; gamma rays pass through both and are only gradually reduced by lead (Co-60: about halved every 12 mm). The counter also picks up background radiation, about 0.5 counts per second here: subtract it from your readings.

Moving the counter away also lowers the rate, roughly as `1/d^2` (the radiation spreads out over a larger area), and for alpha particles there is a sharp cut-off near 4 cm. Use the two unknown sources to identify what they emit. The transmissions are simplified models chosen to give realistic behaviour.
