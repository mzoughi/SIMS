A conceptual model of the atomic nucleus, from one proton up to calcium (20 protons, up to 28 neutrons). Protons (red, +) and neutrons (grey) are drawn as small balls 1.75 fm apart (1 fm = 10−15 m).

Two forces, acting between every pair. All nucleons attract each other through the strong nuclear force, but only when they are very close: in the SIM, only when their dashed circles overlap (centres closer than 2.3 fm, so only nearest neighbours). Beyond that it is zero. Protons also repel each other through the Coulomb (electric) force, which is much weaker at contact (about a tenth of the strong pull of one neighbour) but reaches to any distance, getting weaker as `1/r^2`. Neutrons have no charge. So a neutron only ever attracts: it is the glue. Two protons have a love–hate relationship: they attract when touching but repel at any distance.

Bring in a proton: from far away it feels only a small push that grows as it comes in; the moment its dashed circle overlaps another one, the strong force switches on and quickly wins. The graph shows its energy: the push makes a small hill (the Coulomb barrier) before the deep valley of the strong force. Click any nucleon to see every force on it, pair by pair: green rings mark the neighbours that pull it; for a proton, red dashed rings mark the protons too far away to pull it that still push it. (A very short-range repulsion, not drawn, stops touching nucleons from overlapping, which is why they don’t collapse.) Arrow lengths are on a logarithmic scale so the small Coulomb forces can be seen; the linear option shows the true proportions.

Why bigger nuclei need extra neutrons. Each nucleon is pulled only by its few nearest neighbours, so the strong pull per nucleon soon stops growing. But each proton is pushed by all the other protons, so the repulsion keeps growing with every proton added. Neutrons add pull without push: that is why heavier stable nuclei have more neutrons than protons.

Energy levels. As in an atom, protons and neutrons occupy energy levels, each holding a few of them. Protons and neutrons fill separate ladders. Full shells at the magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28 are especially stable. The level energies shown are schematic.

Stability. With too many neutrons, a neutron turns into a proton (β− decay); with too many protons, a proton turns into a neutron (β+ decay or electron capture); far off balance, the extra nucleon is not bound and leaves at once. Pairing (even numbers) and magic numbers add stability. Half-lives are measured values (rounded); those not in the table are estimated. Binding energies use the liquid-drop formula, a rough estimate for light nuclei. Pair forces: strong `U = -6 s(r)` MeV (`s` = 1 below 1.2 fm, 0 beyond 2.3 fm), Coulomb `U = 1.44//r` MeV (r in fm), `F = -dU//dr`.
