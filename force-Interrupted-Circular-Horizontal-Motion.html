A puck of mass `m` is held by a light rod and spun about a pivot with a constant angular acceleration `alpha`, so its angular speed grows steadily, `omega = alpha t`. Its speed is `v = omega r`, and its velocity is always tangent to the circle.

The acceleration has two parts: a tangential part `a_t = alpha r` that makes the puck speed up, and a centripetal part `a_c = omega^2 r = v^2/r`, pointing toward the pivot, that keeps it on the circle. Only the rod can supply the inward force, its tension: on a horizontal table `T = m omega^2 r`; on a vertical circle gravity helps or hinders, `T = m omega^2 r - m g sin theta`, largest at the bottom.

The rod breaks when the tension needed exceeds its strength (or at a set time). From then on, nothing pulls the puck toward the pivot. On the table no force acts at all: by Newton’s first law the puck moves in a straight line along the tangent, at the speed it had. On the vertical circle only gravity acts: the puck follows a parabola, like any projectile. In neither case does it fly straight outward: there is no outward force.
