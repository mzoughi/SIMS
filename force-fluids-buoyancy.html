Buoyancy: Float, Sink, Dive

A liquid pushes on every surface it touches, and its pressure grows with depth, `p = p_0 + rho g h`. So it pushes harder on the bottom of an object than on its top. The difference is an upward force, the buoyant force. Archimedes’ principle: the buoyant force equals the weight of the liquid the object pushes aside (displaces),

`B = rho_"liquid" g V_"submerged"`.

For a cube of side `a` with its bottom at depth `s`, the pressure difference between bottom and top is `rho g a`, acting on the area `a^2`: `B = rho g a^3`, the weight of a cube of liquid.

1. Float or sink. A cube of mass `m` and side `a` is placed in a tank. Its weight is `W = m g`; its average density is `rho_"obj" = m/a^3`. If `rho_"obj" < rho_"liquid"`, it floats: it sinks until the buoyant force equals its weight, with a fraction `rho_"obj"/rho_"liquid"` of its height below the surface. If `rho_"obj" > rho_"liquid"`, even fully under, `B < W`: it sinks, and the bottom of the tank pushes up with the rest, `N = W - B`. Pushed under and let go, a floating cube bobs up and down before settling (the liquid’s resistance damps the motion).

2. Weighing it in a liquid. The cube hangs from a spring scale and is lowered into a container filled to its spout. The scale reads `T = W - B`: the object seems to weigh less. The liquid pushed out runs into a cup on a second scale: it weighs exactly `B`. So the two readings always add up to the weight of the object, as long as the string is tight.

3. Submarine. A submarine controls its weight, not its volume. Its ballast tanks are flooded with sea water to dive (weight larger than the buoyant force), and emptied with compressed air to rise (weight smaller). With exactly the right amount of water it is neutrally buoyant: `W = B`, and it stays at any depth. The pressure outside grows by about 1 atmosphere every 10 m; below its crush depth the hull would fail. Model (simplified, a mid-sized submarine): hull volume 2000 m³, 10 m high; mass without ballast 1750 t; ballast tanks 400 m³. The water’s resistance grows as the square of the speed. Moving from salt water to fresh water (less dense), the buoyant force drops: the submarine must pump out some ballast.

g = 9.81 m/s². Drawings are not to scale where noted.
