In 1818 Fresnel submitted his wave theory of light to a prize competition of the French Academy. Poisson, a judge who favoured light as particles, worked out a consequence he found absurd: in the shadow of a round opaque disk, the wave theory predicts a bright spot at the very centre. Arago did the experiment and found the spot. It is now called Poisson’s spot, or Arago’s spot, and it was a decisive victory for the wave theory.

Why it happens. Every point of the rim of the disk is at the same distance from the centre of the shadow, so the wavelets coming around the edge all arrive there in phase and add up. Here the screen is not “far away” compared with `a^2/lambda`, so this is Fresnel (near-field) diffraction, not the Fraunhofer pattern of the circular-aperture simulation. The important number is the Fresnel number

`N_F = a^2/(lambda z)`,

roughly the number of Fresnel zones covered by the disk (radius `a`, screen distance `z`). On the axis, the calculation gives exactly the intensity of the uncovered wave, `I = I_0`, whatever the size of the disk. Near the axis the spot follows `I ~~ I_0 J_0^2((2 pi a r)/(lambda z))`, so its radius is about `0.38 lambda z/a`: a few hundredths of a millimetre for a millimetre-size disk. Around the edge of the shadow you see Fresnel fringes.

Babinet’s principle. A hole and a disk of the same size are complementary: their fields add up to the unobstructed wave. For the hole, the centre is bright or dark depending on whether it uncovers an odd or even number of zones, `I = 4 I_0 sin^2(pi N_F/2)`; the disk always gives a bright centre. In practice the disk must be very round and smooth (a ball bearing works well), and the light must be spatially coherent (a laser). The calculation uses the Fresnel approximation for a plane wave.
