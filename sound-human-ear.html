The Human Ear — From Sound Wave to Understanding

The ear turns tiny pressure changes in the air into electrical signals for the brain. The outer ear collects sound, the middle ear amplifies it mechanically, and the inner ear (cochlea) sorts it by frequency and converts it into nerve signals. Click a part, or send a sound wave through the ear.

Energy along the path

Air (pressure wave) → eardrum (mechanical vibration) → ossicles (lever) → oval window → cochlear fluid (fluid wave) → hair cells (electrical signal) → auditory nerve → brainstem → auditory cortex.

The ear detects pressure changes as small as about 20 micropascals, about one five-billionth of atmospheric pressure, moving the eardrum by less than the width of an atom.

Sound passing straight from air into the fluid of the cochlea would be mostly reflected. The middle ear is an impedance matcher: a large eardrum pushes a small oval window through a bony lever, raising the pressure. Change the areas and the lever ratio.

The physics
F₁ = P₁A₁  ·  F₂ = L F₁  ·  P₂ = F₂/A₂
P₂/P₁ = L (A₁/A₂)  ·  gain = 20 log₁₀(P₂/P₁) dB

Air and water have very different acoustic impedances, so only about 0.1% of the sound power would enter the fluid directly (a loss of about 30 dB). The pressure gain of the middle ear recovers most of that loss.

Two tiny muscles (stapedius and tensor tympani) stiffen the chain in response to loud sounds, giving some protection; the reflex takes tens of milliseconds, too slow for sudden bangs.

Inside the cochlea, the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex. Each frequency makes a travelling wave that peaks at its own place: high pitch near the base, low pitch near the apex. Hair cells at the peak send the signal.
The numbers
How pitch is sorted

The uncoiled cochlea is about 35 mm long. The place of the peak follows the Greenwood map, roughly logarithmic in frequency:

f = 165.4 (102.1x − 0.88) Hz,  x = fraction of the length from the apex

Each octave takes about the same length of membrane, which is why musical pitch feels evenly spaced on a logarithmic scale.

Every sound travels past the base on its way to its own place, so hair cells at the base work hardest. That is one reason high frequencies are lost first. Start the tone with a low volume.

An audiogram plots the quietest tone a person can hear at each frequency. Lower on the chart means a louder sound is needed. Choose a profile to see how different problems show up, or do not show up.

Every 10 dB is ten times the intensity, every 3 dB doubles it, so the safe time halves for every 3 dB above 85 dB.

Ringing (tinnitus) or muffled hearing after loud noise are warning signs. Keep earphones below about 60% volume.

Hearing is not the same as listening. In auditory processing disorder (APD, also called central auditory processing disorder, CAPD) the ear usually works normally and the audiogram is often normal, but the brainstem and auditory cortex have difficulty separating, ordering and interpreting sounds, especially speech in noise and fast timing cues.
Keep the volume low. Headphones work best.

The physics: time resolution

The auditory system behaves like a detector with a finite integration time τ. A silence much shorter than τ is smoothed over, and two bursts blend into one, just as a slow sensor cannot resolve two rapid pulses.

processed envelope: dy/dt = (x − y)/τ

Speech depends on such short events: the silent closure before p, t, k lasts tens of milliseconds, and the voice-onset time that separates “ba” from “pa” differs by about 20 to 40 ms.

What APD can feel like

Hearing that someone is talking but not catching the words in a noisy room or an echoing hallway; losing track of fast speech or long spoken instructions; mixing up similar-sounding words; finding it hard to follow one voice when two people talk at once; difficulty telling where a sound comes from; being tired after a lot of listening.

Testing and what helps

An audiologist first rules out ordinary hearing loss, then uses tests such as gap detection, speech in noise, dichotic listening (different words in each ear at once), pattern tests (order of high and low tones) and localization. A pattern across several tests, not one score, points to APD.

Helps: less background noise and echo, sitting close to and facing the speaker, remote-microphone systems, captions and written instructions, speakers who slow down and pause. Making sound louder alone does not fix it, because loudness is not the problem.

The ear converts pressure waves in air into nerve signals in three stages. The outer ear collects sound; the ear canal, a pipe about 2.5 cm long closed at one end, resonates near `f = v/(4L) ~~ 3.4` kHz. The middle ear (eardrum and the three ossicles) is an impedance matcher: a large eardrum drives a small oval window through a lever, giving a pressure gain `P_2/P_1 = L A_1/A_2`, about 22 times or 27 dB, which recovers most of the energy that would otherwise be reflected at an air–fluid boundary.

In the cochlea, the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, so each frequency produces a travelling wave that peaks at its own place (high frequencies near the base, low near the apex), roughly logarithmically in frequency. Hair cells at the peak convert the motion into nerve signals that travel through the auditory nerve, brainstem and thalamus to the auditory cortex.

The audiogram tab compares conductive loss (an air–bone gap), sensorineural loss (noise-induced notch near 4 kHz, age-related high-frequency slope) and auditory processing disorder, which usually shows a normal audiogram. The noise tab uses `beta = 10 log(I/I_0)` and the NIOSH limit `T = 8/2^((L-85)/3)` hours.

Auditory processing disorder (APD, or CAPD) is a difficulty in how the central auditory system processes sound rather than in detecting it. The APD tab models temporal resolution as a smoothing of the sound envelope with a time constant `tau`: a silent gap is heard only if the smoothed envelope dips below a threshold. It also shows speech understanding falling as background noise rises, sooner for the APD listener. The values are illustrative, not clinical, and the SIM cannot diagnose any hearing condition.
