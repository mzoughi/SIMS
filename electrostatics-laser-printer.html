The steps

Laser printers and photocopiers both use xerography, invented by Chester Carlson in 1938. Its heart is a drum coated with a photoconductor: an insulator in the dark, a conductor where light falls on it. The drum turns past a ring of stations:

1. Charge. A corona wire or a charge roller sprays ions onto the drum and charges its whole surface uniformly, typically to about −600 V. 2. Expose. In a laser printer, a laser beam swept across the drum by a spinning polygon mirror switches on wherever the page should be black; in an analogue copier, a lamp lights the original and a lens images it onto the drum, so light reaches the drum wherever the original is white. Where light falls, the photoconductor conducts and the charge leaks to the grounded aluminium core: the drum now carries an invisible latent image of charge. 3. Develop. Toner, a fine powder of plastic and pigment, is charged by rubbing against carrier beads or a blade (triboelectric charging). In a laser printer the toner is negative, and the developer roller is biased at about −400 V: toner is pushed onto the exposed areas (about −100 V) and pushed away from the charged ones (discharged-area development). In an analogue copier the toner is positive and is attracted to the areas that are still charged (charged-area development).

4. Transfer. A roller behind the paper, charged opposite to the toner, pulls the toner from the drum onto the paper. 5. Fuse. Hot rollers (about 200 °C) melt the plastic in the toner and press it into the fibres: the page comes out warm. 6. Clean. A blade scrapes off the few percent of toner that did not transfer. 7. Erase. A lamp floods the drum with light to remove any remaining charge, ready for the next turn. Skip the cleaning, and the left-over toner prints again one drum circumference later: a ghost.

The photoconductor is layered: a thin charge-generation layer absorbs the light and makes electron-hole pairs; the strong field from the surface charge pulls the holes up through a thicker charge-transport layer to cancel the surface charge, while the electrons go to the grounded aluminium. The surface potential falls roughly as `V = V_r + (V_0 - V_r) e^(-E//E_0)` with the light energy `E` received (the photo-induced discharge curve). A toner particle of charge `q` near the drum feels `F = qE_(field)`, with the field set by the difference between the drum and developer potentials.

Colour. Colour printers print four separations, one per toner: cyan, magenta, yellow and black (CMYK), from four drums in a row (a tandem engine) onto a belt or the paper. The toners are subtractive: each absorbs one colour from the white light reflected by the paper (cyan absorbs red, magenta green, yellow blue), so cyan and magenta make blue, yellow and magenta red, and all three a muddy dark brown. Black toner gives a true black with less toner. Shades are made by halftoning: dots of each colour, on screens set at different angles so they do not form moiré patterns, cover more or less of the paper. If one drum is out of line, its colour shifts: a registration error.

Voltages, speeds and efficiencies are typical values; the drum has been drawn larger and slower than a real one, which turns many times per second.
