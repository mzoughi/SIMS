The Flight of a Frisbee

a spinning wing · lift and drag keep it up · spin keeps it from flipping over

Throws

A frisbee is a wing: a thin disc, slightly curved on top, that flies at a small angle of attack `alpha` (the tilt of the disc relative to its direction of motion). Three forces act on it: its weight `mg`, the lift `L` at right angles to its motion, and the drag `D` against its motion,

`L = 1/2 rho v^2 A C_L`,   `D = 1/2 rho v^2 A C_D`,

with `A` the area of the disc. The model is the one fitted to wind-tunnel and flight measurements of a 175 g Ultimate disc (Hummel, 2003; Potts and Crowther, 2002): `C_L ~~ 0.15 + 1.4 alpha`, `C_D ~~ 0.08 + 2.72 (alpha + 4°)^2` (`alpha` in radians), and a pitching moment `C_M ~~ -0.015 + 0.16 alpha` (from the wind-tunnel data of Potts and Crowther; it is small, and changes sign near 5°). The disc is 27 cm across.

1. Throw it. Choose a throw, or set the launch yourself: speed, launch angle, the tilt of the disc (nose up or down), its bank (left or right side down) and its spin. The disc’s path is shown from the side and from above. A flat, fast throw at a small angle of attack goes farthest: little drag, and enough lift to stay up. A throw aimed high climbs, stalls, and comes down steeply. An upside-down throw gets almost no lift from its curved shape: it falls much like a thrown ball. A throw with no spin pitches up, stalls and flips. The dots are the measured paths of three gentle backyard throws filmed by John Leeman (regular, upside-down and no-spin “stall” throws, about 5 m/s).

2. Why it must spin. The lift does not act at the centre of the disc but a little ahead of it, so it exerts a torque `tau` that tries to tip the front up. Without spin, the disc tips over (pitches up), stalls and falls, as in Leeman’s no-spin throw. A spinning disc has an angular momentum `vec L_s = I omega`, along its axis. The torque changes it, `Delta vec L_s = vec tau Delta t`, but `vec tau` is sideways to `vec L_s`, so the axis turns sideways slowly instead of tipping: the disc slowly banks, like a gyroscope precessing, at the rate

`Omega = tau/(I omega)`.

The faster the spin, the slower it banks; the banked disc then curves to one side (a right-handed backhand throw, spinning clockwise seen from above, fades to the left at the end of its flight).

3. Where the lift comes from. As in the airplane-wing SIM, the disc turns the air flowing past it downward; by Newton’s third law the air pushes it up. Each second it meets a mass of air `Delta m/Delta t ~~ rho v D h` (`D` the disc’s width, `h` the thickness of the layer of air it affects, above and below) and gives it a downward speed `Delta v`; the force is `L = (Delta m/Delta t) Delta v` (Landell-Mills, 2020). Equivalently, the air flows faster over the curved top than under the bottom, so the pressure is lower above (Bernoulli). In level flight the lift must equal the disc’s weight, `mg` ≈ 1.7 N. The spin itself adds little lift; its job is to keep the disc stable.

Air density 1.225 kg/m³. The model is a simplification: it ignores the wobble of the disc and treats the effect of spin on the aerodynamic forces as negligible.
