How the scan works

Many nuclei, including the proton of hydrogen, have a spin and behave like tiny magnets. In a magnetic field `B` they have two possible energies: lower when aligned with the field, higher when opposed. The energy difference is proportional to the field, `Delta E = h f_0` with `f_0 = (gamma/(2 pi)) B`; for hydrogen `gamma/(2 pi) = 42.58` MHz/T, so `f_0 ~~ 64` MHz at 1.5 T, a radio frequency.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR): radio photons are absorbed, flipping nuclei to the higher level, only when their energy matches the gap, `hf = Delta E`. The excited nuclei then relax back and re-emit radio waves at the same frequency, which a detector picks up. Each kind of nucleus has its own `gamma`, so each resonates at its own frequency.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses the hydrogen in the water and fat of the body. Gradient coils add a small field that changes across the body, `B = B_0 + G_x x + G_y y`, so the resonance frequency depends on position: the frequency of the signal tells where it comes from. Repeating the measurement with gradients in many directions and combining the profiles reconstructs an image (projection reconstruction, as in the first MRI images). In this simplified model a tumor shows up because it contains more hydrogen; real scanners also use differences in relaxation times.
