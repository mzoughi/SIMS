Static in the Clothes Dryer

a tumbling load is a triboelectric generator · moisture drains charge · dryer sheets fight back

INSIDE THE DRYER
The load
The triboelectric series

1. Inside the dryer. A tumbling load is a triboelectric generator, like the belt of a Van de Graaff generator. Each time two garments touch and separate, electrons move from one surface to the other: the fabric nearer the positive end of the triboelectric series (nylon, wool, silk) gives up electrons and becomes positive; the fabric nearer the negative end (polyester, acrylic) takes them and becomes negative. Cotton sits near the middle. The charge per contact grows with the distance between the two fabrics in the series.

A load of a single fabric hardly charges: identical surfaces have no reason to exchange electrons. The little charge they get comes from rubbing on the metal drum, and it has the same sign on every garment, so the garments repel rather than cling. A mixed load charges with opposite signs, and opposite charges attract: the garments cling to each other and, charged strongly enough, to the drum.

Moisture matters most. Water on and in the fibres conducts, so while the clothes are damp, charge drains away almost as soon as it is made: the decay time of surface charge is a small fraction of a second. As the load dries, the surface resistance rises by many powers of ten and the decay time grows to minutes: the charge builds up. That is why static appears at the end of the cycle, why over-drying makes it worse, why it is worst in dry winter air, and why a moisture sensor that stops the dryer when the clothes are just dry helps.

2. Dryer sheets. A dryer sheet is a non-woven fabric coated with a fabric softener (a cationic surfactant, such as a quaternary ammonium compound) mixed with a fatty material that melts in the warm drum and spreads onto the clothes. The softener acts in three ways: its positive heads bind to the negatively charged sites on the fibres and neutralize them; its long oily tails make the fibre surface slippery, so rubbing transfers far less charge; and the coating attracts a thin film of moisture that makes the surface more conductive, so the charge that does form leaks away. Together they cut the static by a factor of a hundred or more.

3. Unloading. Two clinging garments of area `A` with charges `+Q` and `-Q` attract like the plates of a capacitor: `F = Q^2/(2 epsilon_0 A)`. Pulling them apart increases the separation `d`, lowers the capacitance `C ~~ epsilon_0 A/d + C_0`, and raises the voltage `V = Q/C`, to thousands of volts. Where the field at the tips of fibres exceeds the breakdown field of air (about 3 MV/m), tiny discharges crackle, and in a dark room they flash.

The charges, the decay times and the drying rate are typical, approximate values chosen to show the trends; real triboelectric series differ between sources, and depend on surface finishes and humidity.
