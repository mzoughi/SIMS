The series, step by step
Counting the decays

Nuclei with `Z > 82` are unstable and decay naturally. When a heavy nucleus decays to a lighter one, the daughter nucleus can become the parent of the next decay, and so on. This produces a long chain of decays called a decay series, which ends with a stable nucleus.

As in Figures 12.5.3 to 12.5.5, the neutron number `N = A - Z` is plotted upward and the atomic number `Z` across. An alpha decay, `\ _Z^A X -> \ _(Z-2)^(A-4) X' + \ _2^4 He`, lowers both `Z` and `N` by 2: a red arrow going down and to the left. A beta decay, `\ _Z^A X -> \ _(Z+1)^A X' + \ _(-1)^0 e + bar nu`, raises `Z` by 1 and lowers `N` by 1: a blue arrow going down and to the right.

Because only alpha decay changes the mass number, and by exactly 4, the mass numbers of a series all leave the same remainder when divided by 4. There are therefore four series: thorium-232 (`4n`, ending at Pb-208), neptunium-237 (`4n + 1`, ending at Bi-209), uranium-238 (`4n + 2`, ending at Pb-206) and uranium-235 (`4n + 3`, ending at Pb-207). The numbers of decays follow from the two ends: `n_alpha = (A_(start) - A_(end))/4` and `n_beta = Z_(end) - Z_(start) + 2 n_alpha`. Thorium-232 and uranium-238 have half-lives comparable to or longer than the age of the Earth, so their series are still found in nature; neptunium-237 has decayed away and is made in the laboratory. Some nuclei can decay by either alpha or beta emission (branches); both routes lead to the same end. Half-lives are those of the figures, rounded.
