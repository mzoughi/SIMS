Lift and Drag

drag pulls back along the airflow · lift pushes across it · both grow as ½ ρ v² A

Controls

When an object moves through air, the air pushes on it. That push is split into two parts: drag `D`, along the direction of the airflow, which slows the object, and lift `L`, across the airflow (at right angles to it). Both grow with the square of the speed:

`D = 1/2 rho C_D A v^2`,   `L = 1/2 rho C_L A v^2`,

where `rho` = 1.20 kg/m³ is the density of air, `A` a reference area, and `C_D`, `C_L` the drag and lift coefficients, which depend on the shape and on how the object meets the air. Lift is not always “up”: it is at right angles to the airflow, and can point down.

Where lift comes from. An object gets lift when it turns the air that flows past it. A wing tilted at an angle of attack `alpha`, a tilted frisbee, or a spinning ball all push air downward behind them (the “downwash”); by Newton’s third law, the air pushes them up. Equivalently, the air flows faster over the top, where the pressure is lower, than under the bottom.

1. Airplane wing. A small airplane (1000 kg, wing area 16.2 m²) meets the air at an angle of attack `alpha`. The lift coefficient grows about linearly with `alpha`, by about 0.1 per degree, until the wing stalls near 15°: the air no longer follows the top of the wing, it breaks away into swirls, and the lift drops while the drag shoots up. The drag coefficient grows with lift (pushing more air down costs more). In level flight, lift equals weight, `1/2 rho C_L A v^2 = m g`: a slow plane needs a large angle of attack, a fast one a small angle. The engine’s thrust (not shown) balances the drag.

2. Frisbee. A flying disc (175 g, 27.4 cm across) is a spinning wing. Its spin keeps its tilt steady, like a gyroscope; without spin it would flip and flutter. It is thrown with its nose tilted up by an angle of attack; the coefficients are those measured by Hummel (2003): `C_L = 0.15 + 1.4 alpha`, `C_D = 0.08 + 2.72 (alpha + 0.07)^2` (`alpha` in radians). As it slows and starts to fall, the air meets it from below, the angle of attack grows, and so do lift and drag.

3. Spinning balls. A spinning ball drags air around with it; with backspin the air leaving the ball is thrown downward, so the ball is pushed up (the Magnus effect); topspin pushes it down. The lift coefficient depends on the spin ratio `S = r omega // v` (how fast the surface turns compared with the speed): about `1.5 S` for small `S`, `0.09 + 0.6 S` above `S = 0.1`. Drag coefficients: golf 0.25 (its dimples lower the drag), baseball 0.35, soccer 0.25, tennis 0.55 (fuzzy), ping-pong 0.50. The spin is taken as constant during the flight.
