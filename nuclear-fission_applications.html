Breeder reactors

Some fast neutrons are absorbed by U-238, which turns into fissionable plutonium-239:

10n + 23892U → 23992U → 23993Np → 23994Pu

(two β− decays). Since 99.3% of natural uranium is U-238, breeding could stretch the supply of fuel about 100 times. Drawbacks: plutonium is a serious health hazard if released, and it can be used to build bombs (nuclear proliferation).

A fission chain reaction can proceed uncontrollably, as in an explosion, or in a controlled manner, as in a nuclear reactor at a power plant.

The atomic bomb. The sample must be massive enough that a typical neutron is more likely to induce fission than to escape; the minimum mass is the critical mass. The original design brought together two pieces of U-235, each below the critical mass: one, shaped as a bullet, is fired into the other. Plutonium-239, more fissionable, was used in a design where pieces at the edge of a sphere are driven to the centre simultaneously by chemical explosions. Once above the critical mass, the number of fissions grows exponentially. The bomb tab is a schematic two-dimensional model; nothing is to scale.

The nuclear reactor. In a pressurized water reactor, water slows the neutrons so that they can split U-235, and carries the heat to a steam generator; the steam turns a turbine and a generator. Cadmium control rods absorb neutrons and adjust the rate, keeping the multiplication factor `k = 1`. If the water boils away, the chain reaction stops, because the water is needed to slow the neutrons. Breeder reactors also turn U-238 into fissionable Pu-239. The reactor tab uses a simplified model of the power and temperature (`dP/dt prop (k - 1) P`) chosen for clear, watchable behaviour.
