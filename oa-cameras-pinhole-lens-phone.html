Cameras: from the Pinhole to the Phone

This simulation builds a camera up from nothing, and ends with the cameras in a phone.

1. Pinhole. Without anything in front of the sensor, every point of the scene lights every pixel: no image. A pinhole lets through only a thin beam from each point, so the beams land in separate places, forming an upside-down image. A smaller hole gives a sharper image, but only up to a point: diffraction spreads the light by about `2.44 lambda d / p` for a hole of diameter `p` a distance `d` from the sensor, so the sharpest image comes from `p ~~ sqrt(2.44 lambda d)`. And a pinhole lets in very little light.

2. Lens. A lens bends the whole cone of light from each point back to a point: sharp and bright. A thin lens of focal length `f` forms the image of an object at distance `s` at

`1/s + 1/(s') = 1/f`.

Objects at other distances focus in front of or behind the sensor, and appear as small discs, circles of confusion, of diameter `c = (f^2/N) |s - s_f| / (s (s_f - f))` for a lens focused at `s_f`. The range of distances that look sharp is the depth of field. The aperture, an iris behind the lens, sets the f-number `N = f/D`: a larger `N` (smaller hole) lets in less light but gives a greater depth of field. A longer focal length magnifies more and sees a narrower field: `"FOV" = 2 arctan(w/(2f))` for a sensor of width `w`.

3. Exposure. The light collected depends on the aperture (as `1/N^2`), the shutter time `t`, and the sensor's sensitivity, the ISO speed `S`. Photographers count them in stops, each a factor of 2: the aperture value `AV = 2 log_2 N`, the time value `TV = log_2 (1/t)`, the speed value `S_v = log_2 (S/3.125)`. A long shutter time blurs moving things and a shaking hand; a high ISO amplifies the signal and the noise.

4. Sensor. The sensor turns light into charge, pixel by pixel. Light arrives as photons, and their number fluctuates: a pixel that collects `n` photons has a noise of about `sqrt n`, so its signal-to-noise ratio is `sqrt n`. Bigger pixels collect more photons and are less noisy, which is why large sensors give cleaner pictures. Each pixel sees only one colour, through a red, green or blue filter arranged in a Bayer pattern; the camera's colour engine fills in the missing colours (demosaicing), reduces the noise, sets the white balance and the tones, and saves a JPEG.

5. Phone. A phone is only about 8 mm thick, so its cameras must be tiny: tiny sensors and focal lengths of a few millimetres. There is no room for a zoom lens, whose groups of lenses must move over a long distance, so phones carry several fixed cameras -- ultra-wide, main and telephoto -- and switch between them. Their focal lengths are quoted as the equivalent for a full-frame camera: `f_"eq" = f xx` the crop factor (43.3 mm divided by the sensor's diagonal). The telephoto's real focal length is longer than the phone is thick, so its light is turned sideways by a prism and runs along the phone: a periscope. Between the cameras, the phone crops the picture (digital zoom), which throws away pixels. Tiny sensors give a large depth of field, so the blurred background of a portrait is made by computation, from a depth map; and night mode combines many frames, cutting the noise by the square root of their number.
