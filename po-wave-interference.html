Two waves travel along the same line, in the same direction and at the same speed `v`. Each has its own amplitude `A`, frequency `f` and starting phase `phi`:

`y_1 = A_1 cos(2 pi f_1 t - k_1 x + phi_1)`,   `y_2 = A_2 cos(2 pi f_2 t - k_2 x + phi_2)`,   `k = (2 pi f)/v`.

The first view shows each wave on its own, the second shows both on the same axis, and the third shows what is really there: their sum, added point by point (the principle of superposition), `y = y_1 + y_2`. This is interference.

Same frequency. The sum is a wave of the same frequency. If the waves are in step (`Delta phi = 0, 360^@, ...`) their crests line up and the amplitudes add: constructive interference, `A = A_1 + A_2`. If they are half a cycle apart (`Delta phi = 180^@`) a crest meets a trough and they subtract: destructive interference, `A = |A_1 - A_2|`, zero when the amplitudes are equal. In general

`A = sqrt(A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2 A_1 A_2 cos Delta phi)`,

which the phasors show: each wave is an arrow of length `A` turning once per period, and the sum is the sum of the arrows.

Different frequencies. The two waves drift in and out of step, so the sum swells and fades: beats, at the beat frequency `f_"beat" = |f_1 - f_2|`. Against time, at one place, you hear (or see) the loudness rise and fall `|f_1 - f_2|` times per second.
