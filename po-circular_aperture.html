This simulation shows the diffraction of light through a small circular hole of diameter `D`. Light of wavelength `lambda` passes through the hole and falls on a screen a distance `L` away. Instead of a sharp spot, the screen shows a bright central disk, the Airy disk, surrounded by faint concentric rings.

The pattern is like the single-slit pattern turned around the axis, but the geometry of a circle changes the numbers. The intensity is `I = I_0 ((2 J_1(x))/x)^2` with `x = (pi D sin theta)/lambda`, where `J_1` is a Bessel function. The first dark ring is at

`sin theta_1 = 1.22 lambda/D`,

a little further out than the first minimum of a slit of width `D` (`sin theta = lambda/D`): tick the comparison box to see both. The next dark rings are at `2.23 lambda/D` and `3.24 lambda/D`. On the screen the Airy disk has a radius `r ~~ 1.22 lambda L/D` and contains 84% of the light; the first bright ring has only 1.75% of the central intensity, which is why the magnification buttons (which also brighten the face-on view) are needed to see it.

A smaller hole or a longer wavelength makes a larger disk. This sets the sharpness of every lens, eye and telescope: a point of light is always imaged as an Airy disk, which limits how close two points can be and still be seen as separate (the Rayleigh criterion, in the next simulation).
