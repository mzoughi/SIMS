e = 1.602×10−19 C; 1 u = 1.661×10−27 kg. A proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron, so almost all the mass of an atom is in its nucleus.

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus made of protons (charge `+e`) and neutrons (no charge), surrounded by much lighter electrons (charge `-e`). The nucleus is about `10^(-15)` m across, the atom about `10^(-10)` m: if the atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea at the centre. Yet the nucleus holds almost all the mass, since a proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron.

An atom is written `\ _Z^A X`: `Z`, the atomic number, is the number of protons and decides the element; `A`, the mass number, is the number of protons plus neutrons, `A = Z + N`. A neutral atom has `Z` electrons.

Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, for example `\ _6^12 C`, `\ _6^13 C` and `\ _6^14 C`. Their electrons are the same, so they behave the same way chemically, but their masses differ and some are radioactive. Ions have the same nucleus but a different number of electrons: losing electrons gives a positive ion (`Na^+`), gaining them a negative ion (`Cl^-`); the charge is written at the top right. Changing the number of protons changes the element itself, which only happens in nuclear reactions.

The electrons are shown either as a cloud, the modern picture, which shows where they are likely to be found, or as shells (the Bohr model), which fill from the inside out (here following the usual filling order; a few elements are exceptions). Sizes are not to scale.
