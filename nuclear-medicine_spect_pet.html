Single-photon-emission computed tomography (SPECT). The tag emits single gamma photons (Tc-99m: 0.140 MeV) in all directions. A gamma camera sees only the photons that pass straight through the holes of its lead collimator; it rotates around the patient, recording one projection at each angle, and a computer reconstructs the slice, as in X-ray CT. Positron emission tomography (PET). The tag decays by β+ emission. The positron travels a millimetre or so, meets an electron, and the two annihilate: e+ + e− → 2γ, two 0.511-MeV photons going in opposite directions. A ring of detectors records both at the same time (a coincidence), so the annihilation lies on the line joining the two detectors. No collimator is needed: PET is more sensitive than SPECT and its resolution is better, about 0.5 cm.

A radiopharmaceutical contains an unstable isotope. Its chemistry, which does not depend on which isotope is present, decides where it concentrates in the body; detectors outside the body locate it from its radiation. Such drugs are called radioactive tags because they let doctors track the drug: iodine concentrates in the thyroid, TlCl follows the blood like NaCl, colloids are taken up by the liver, phosphonates by bone. About 80% of procedures use `\ ^(99m) Tc`, which emits a single 0.142-MeV gamma ray and has a short 6.0-h half-life. The activity is often given in millicuries, 1 mCi = `3.7 xx 10^7` Bq, and the number of nuclei is `N = A t_(1/2)/0.693`. The tag also leaves the organ by biological processes, so its activity falls with an effective half-life, `1/T_(eff) = 1/T_(phys) + 1/T_(bio)`.

SPECT (single-photon-emission CT) uses a rotating gamma camera with a lead collimator that accepts only photons travelling straight toward it; projections at many angles are combined into a three-dimensional image, as in X-ray CT. Its resolution is about 1 cm.

PET (positron emission tomography) uses `beta^+` emitters such as `\ ^11 C`, `\ ^13 N`, `\ ^15 O` and `\ ^18 F`, which can tag natural body compounds. The positron annihilates with an electron, `e^+ + e^- -> 2 gamma`, giving two 0.511-MeV photons moving in opposite directions; detecting both at once locates the annihilation on a line. Its resolution, about 0.5 cm, is better than that of SPECT. The simulation is simplified: two-dimensional slices, no attenuation, scatter or random coincidences; biological behaviour, modern activities and positron ranges are approximate.
