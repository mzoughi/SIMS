Quick comparisons

Air density 1.20 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s². Each shape falls front first (the front is at the bottom). Changing anything brings the objects back to the top.

Up to three objects fall side by side from the same height, released together from rest. They have the same mass, so the same weight `W = m g`, and the same frontal area `A`, the area the oncoming air meets. Only their shape differs. The drag of the air on each one is

`D = 1/2 rho C_d A v^2`,

where `C_d`, the drag coefficient, carries the effect of the shape. Each object speeds up until its drag equals its weight, at its terminal velocity

`v_t = sqrt((2 m g)/(rho C_d A))`.

So the shape with the smallest `C_d` falls fastest. Since `v_t` goes as `1/sqrt(C_d)`, a shape with four times the drag coefficient falls half as fast.

Why shape matters. The air has to get out of the way of the object and close up again behind it. A blunt shape (a flat plate, a cube) leaves a wide, swirling wake behind it, a region of low pressure that holds the object back: large `C_d`. A streamlined shape lets the air close up smoothly behind it, leaving a thin wake: small `C_d`. The tail matters as much as the nose: the cone and the streamlined body have similar fronts, but the streamlined body's tapered tail cuts the drag by more than ten times. An open cup facing the air, like a parachute, catches the air and has the largest `C_d` here.

Drag coefficients used (measured, at speeds where they are nearly constant; Hoerner, as reproduced on Wikipedia and in engineering tables): streamlined body 0.04, half-sphere (round side first) 0.42, sphere 0.47, cone (point first) 0.50, angled cube (edge first) 0.80, long cylinder (end first) 0.82, cube (face first) 1.05, short cylinder (end first) 1.15, flat disk 1.17, open cup facing the air (parachute) 1.42. Real values change somewhat with speed and size; a sphere’s can drop sharply at high speed.
