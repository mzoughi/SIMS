Biological Effects of Nuclear Radiation

Nuclear radiation damages cells by ionizing atoms as it passes through them (Figure 12.8.5). The DNA in each cell can repair most damage, but damage that is close together is much harder to repair. A cell that cannot repair its DNA may become dormant (senescence), commit suicide (programmed cell death), or divide out of control, possibly leading to cancer.

Why closely packed damage is worse
The absorbed dose is the energy deposited per kilogram: 1 rad = 0.01 J/kg (the SI unit is the gray, 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad). To account for how damaging each kind of radiation is, the dose is multiplied by the relative biological effectiveness (RBE), giving the dose in rem; in SI units, the sievert, 1 Sv = 100 rem.

Nuclear radiation damages cells by ionizing atoms as it passes through them, which can alter the DNA. Cells repair most damage; if repair fails, a cell may become dormant (senescence), die (programmed cell death), or divide without control, possibly leading to cancer. Radiation that ionizes densely, such as alpha particles, concentrates its damage and is harder to repair than sparsely ionizing gamma rays.

The absorbed dose is measured in rad, `1 text( rad) = 0.01 text( J/kg)`. Multiplying by the relative biological effectiveness gives the dose in rem, `text(rem) = text(rad) xx RBE`, and `1 text( Sv) = 100 text( rem)`. Below 100 mSv is a low dose, 0.1 to 1 Sv moderate, above 1 Sv high. The immediate effects of a single whole-body dose range from none (below 0.1 Sv) through blood changes, nausea and hair loss to death (4.5 Sv kills half of those exposed without treatment).

Everyone receives a few millisieverts per year from natural background radiation (cosmic rays, soil and building materials, radon, and isotopes inside the body) and from medical exams. The cell model is illustrative: ionization counts and repair probabilities are chosen to show the trend, not measured values. Altitude and flight doses are rough estimates.
