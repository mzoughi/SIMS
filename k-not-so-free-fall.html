Not So Free Fall

held at a constant velocity, then released: what changes, and what does not?

A ball of mass `m` is first moved at a constant velocity (downward, upward, or held at rest) by a force `vec F` from whatever holds it: a hand, a rope, an engine. A constant velocity means no acceleration, so by Newton’s second law the net force is zero: the holding force exactly balances gravity, `F = m g`, upward, whichever way the ball moves.

When the ball is released, the holding force disappears and only gravity remains: the acceleration jumps at once to `g`, downward. The velocity, however, does not jump: the ball keeps the velocity it had and only then starts to change, `v = v_0 - g (t - t_r)`. Released while moving up, it keeps rising, slows down, stops for an instant at the top, and falls back; released while moving down, it falls faster and faster from the speed it already had.

On the graphs, the position is a straight line while the ball is held (constant velocity) and a parabola after the release; the velocity is flat, then a straight line of slope `-g`; the acceleration is zero, then `-g`.
