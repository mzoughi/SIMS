Radioactive dating uses naturally occurring radioactivity to find the age of a material: estimate the original number of nuclei `N_0` and the present number `N`, then use the decay law `N = N_0 e^(-lambda t)` with the known decay constant to find the time.

In carbon-14 dating, carbon-14 nuclei are produced when high-energy cosmic radiation strikes `\ ^14 N` nuclei in the upper atmosphere; they decay with a half-life of 5730 years. Radioactive carbon has the same chemistry as stable carbon, so it becomes part of every living organism, with an abundance of 1.3 parts per trillion of normal carbon. When the organism dies, carbon exchange with the environment stops and the `\ ^14 C` is no longer replenished as it decays. Comparing the `\ ^14 C` in an artifact with that in living tissue gives the time since death:

`t = -ln(N/N_0)/lambda`,   `lambda = 0.693/T_(1/2) = 1.21 xx 10^(-4)` y`^(-1)`.

Since the activity is proportional to the number of nuclei, `N/N_0` can be found from the activity: living carbon has about 250 Bq per kilogram (Example 12.4.4). Carbon-14 dating can be used for biological tissue as old as about 50 000 years, but is most accurate for younger samples, which still contain more `\ ^14 C`.
