WHAT MAKES A SPARK

1. What makes a spark. A gas is normally an insulator, but it always contains a few free electrons (made by cosmic rays and natural radioactivity). In an electric field `E` an electron gains energy `eE lambda` between collisions, where `lambda` is its mean free path (about 0.35 µm in air at 1 atm). Most collisions are gentle, but the fastest electrons can knock an electron out of a molecule (the ionization energy of N₂ is 15.6 eV, of O₂ 12.1 eV). Each new electron is accelerated in turn: an avalanche. Excited molecules emit light and photons start new avalanches ahead of the first; a thin conducting streamer bridges the gap, a large current flows, heats the channel to thousands of kelvin, and we see and hear a spark. The breakdown voltage of a uniform gap depends only on the product of pressure and gap length (Paschen’s law):

`V_b = (B p d)/(ln(A p d) - ln(ln(1 + 1/gamma)))`,

with constants `A`, `B` and `gamma` for each gas. For air at 1 atm this is about 3 kV per millimetre for gaps of a few millimetres or more. It has a minimum (about 330 V for air): at very low `pd` there are too few molecules to collide with, and the voltage rises again. A pointed electrode concentrates the field: the air near the tip breaks down first as a quiet violet corona glow, and the spark comes at a lower average field.

Colours. An excited atom or molecule emits light only at certain wavelengths, the lines of its spectrum; the mix of lines gives the colour. In air most of the light of a weak discharge comes from nitrogen molecules, much of it in the violet and near ultraviolet: corona and faint sparks look violet-blue. Neon glows orange-red, argon lavender, helium pink-peach, hydrogen magenta. A strong spark or lightning is so hot (lightning up to about 30 000 K) that the channel also emits a broad continuum, and many lines of ionized nitrogen and oxygen add up: the light is nearly white with a blue-violet tint. Seen through rain, dust or smoke, blue light is scattered out of the line of sight, so distant lightning can look yellow, orange or red.

No gas (as at the surface of the Moon, about 10−15 atm). Free electrons cross the gap without colliding with anything, so there is no avalanche, no glow and no corona: Paschen’s law gives no breakdown at all. Only an extreme field, here modelled as about `50 sqrt(d)` kV (d in mm), pulls electrons out of the metal (field emission); they heat the electrodes, which evaporate, and the “vacuum arc” then burns in metal vapour and shows the lines of the metal (copper: green and violet-blue), not of a gas. This is why space hardware can hold high voltages that would spark in air, while low-pressure gas (near the Paschen minimum) is the most dangerous.

2. The doorknob. Shuffling on a carpet transfers charge by contact (the triboelectric effect) and leaves the body at several kilovolts (the body is a capacitor of about 150 pF). Near the metal knob the field in the small gap becomes strong enough for breakdown: a spark of a few millimetres carries the charge `Q = CV` in a fraction of a microsecond, with energy `1/2 C V^2`. Thresholds used (approximate): felt above 3 kV, visible above 5 kV, painful above 10 kV. Humid air lets the charge leak away. Touching the knob first with a key moves the spark to the key tip, away from the nerves of the fingertip.

3. Cloud to ground. In a thunderstorm, collisions between ice crystals and soft hail separate charge: the upper cloud becomes positive, the middle negative, with a small positive pocket near the base. The negative charge induces positive charge on the ground below. A faint stepped leader works its way down in steps of tens of metres, branching as it goes; when it nears the ground, upward streamers rise from tall objects. When they connect, a huge current (typically about 30 kA) surges up the channel: the bright return stroke. A dart leader may follow the same channel, giving several strokes in a fraction of a second: the flicker of a flash. The channel is heated to about 30 000 K in microseconds; the air expands explosively, and the shock wave becomes thunder. Light arrives almost at once, sound at 343 m/s: about 3 s per kilometre.

4. Cloud to cloud. Most lightning (roughly three quarters) never reaches the ground: it goes between charge regions inside one cloud (intracloud), between clouds, or crawls along the underside of the anvil (“spider” lightning). Hidden inside the cloud, it lights the whole cloud (“sheet lightning”).

5. Lightning rod. A rod does not attract lightning from far away and does not drain the cloud; it offers the approaching leader a preferred, safe point of attachment, connected to a ground electrode by a heavy conductor. Where a leader attaches is decided when its tip comes within the striking distance `r ~~ 10 I^(0.65)` m (I in kA) of an object; the rolling sphere of radius `r` shows the protected region: places the sphere cannot touch without first touching the rod. Weak strokes have short striking distances and can get past. A sharp tip under a charged cloud also glows with corona (St. Elmo’s fire).

The numbers (Paschen constants, line intensities, body capacitance, charging rates, lightning currents and the striking-distance formula) are typical, approximate values; the animations are slowed down by factors of thousands to millions.
