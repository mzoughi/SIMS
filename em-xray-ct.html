X-rays and CT Scans — Seeing Inside the Body

In an X-ray tube, electrons accelerated through a voltage V slam into a tungsten target. As they are deflected and slowed by the nuclei they emit X-ray photons (bremsstrahlung), plus sharp characteristic lines. The X-rays pass through the patient; tissues that absorb more (bone) let fewer through and appear white on the image: I = I₀e−μx.

A radiograph squashes the whole body onto one picture: structures in front of and behind each other overlap. In computed tomography (CT) the tube and detector rotate around the patient, recording one projection at each angle. A computer then works backward to the slice that produced all of them, giving a map of the attenuation coefficient μ at every point, in Hounsfield units.

X-ray production. In an X-ray tube, electrons from a hot cathode are accelerated through a potential difference `V` and strike a tungsten anode. Decelerated by the nuclei, they emit bremsstrahlung X-rays with a continuous spectrum up to the maximum photon energy `E_(max) = eV`, i.e. a minimum wavelength `lambda_(min) = hc/(eV)`. Electrons with enough energy (over 69.5 keV for tungsten) also knock out inner-shell electrons, producing characteristic lines (`K_alpha` at 59 keV, `K_beta` at 67 keV). A filter removes the low-energy photons, which would only add dose.

Radiography. X-rays passing through a thickness `x` of material are attenuated as `I = I_0 e^(-mu x)`. The attenuation coefficient `mu` is much larger for bone (higher atomic number, through the photoelectric effect) than for soft tissue, and very small for the air-filled lungs, so bone appears white and lungs dark. Lower voltages give more contrast but more dose; fewer photons (lower mAs) give more quantum noise, which can hide small details.

Computed tomography. A radiograph superimposes everything along each ray. A CT scanner measures projections `ln(I_0/I) = int mu\ dx` at many angles around the patient (the sinogram) and reconstructs the attenuation map of a slice by filtered back-projection. Values are expressed in Hounsfield units, `HU = 1000 (mu - mu_(water))/mu_(water)`, from −1000 for air to about +1000 for dense bone, and displayed through a window. A chest CT gives a dose of several millisieverts, hundreds of times a chest radiograph. The simulation uses simplified physics (approximate attenuation data, parallel-beam monochromatic CT); doses are rough estimates.
