What Shape Is a Raindrop?

surface tension pulls it round · the air pushing on it flattens it · too big, and it breaks apart

3. Size, speed and shape

The activity. First draw the shape you think a falling raindrop has, on the drawing pad. Then reveal the real shape, for the size of drop chosen with the slider. Most people draw a teardrop, pointed at the top. Real raindrops never look like that.

Two effects compete. Surface tension pulls the surface of the water tight, like a stretched skin, and tries to make the drop a sphere, the shape with the least surface for its volume. It squeezes the water with a pressure of about `2 gamma / r` (`gamma` = 0.072 N/m for water): the smaller the drop, the stronger this squeeze. The air rushing past the falling drop pushes hardest on its bottom, where it stops the air, with a pressure of about `1/2 rho_"air" v^2`; on the sides, where the air flows fast, the pressure is lower, and the drop is pulled out sideways.

squash number `= (1/2 rho_"air" v^2)/(2 gamma // r)`

compares the two. Small drops (less than about 2 mm) fall slowly and have a strong surface squeeze: the squash number is small and they stay spheres. Medium drops (2 to 5 mm) fall faster: they are flattened at the bottom, rounded on top, like a hamburger bun; the biggest ones have a dent in the bottom. Drops larger than about 5 mm are not stable: the air pushes the dent in until the drop blows up into an upside-down bag, like a parachute, with a thick rim; the bag bursts and the rim breaks into smaller drops. This is why raindrops are almost never larger than about 5 mm.

Speed. A drop falls at its terminal speed, where the drag of the air balances its weight. The graph compares the measured speeds of real raindrops (Gunn and Kinzer, 1949) with the model of a rigid sphere (drag coefficient 0.47, as in the air-resistance SIM). For small drops they agree; for large drops the flattened shape catches more air, so real drops fall slower, leveling off near 9 m/s.
