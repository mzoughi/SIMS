Adding Waves: the Principle of Superposition

When two waves travel through the same medium, the displacement at each point is the sum of the displacements the two waves would cause on their own: y = y₁ + y₂. The top graph shows wave 1, the middle graph wave 2, and the bottom graph their sum. Move the yellow reading line to any point and check that the three values add up.

Each wave is a sinusoidal wave on a string, `y_i(x,t) = A_i sin(k_i x -+ omega_i t + phi_i)`, with amplitude `A_i`, wavelength `lambda_i` (`k_i = 2pi//lambda_i`), speed `v_i` (`omega_i = 2pi v_i//lambda_i`, so `f_i = v_i//lambda_i`) and phase constant `phi_i`; the sign is `-` for a wave moving to the right and `+` for a wave moving to the left.

The principle of superposition: when both waves are present, the displacement is `y = y_1 + y_2` at every point and every instant. Where the two displacements have the same sign they add (constructive interference); where they have opposite signs they partly or completely cancel (destructive interference). Two identical waves in phase give a wave of twice the amplitude; in antiphase (`phi_2 - phi_1 = 180 deg`) they cancel everywhere. Two identical waves moving in opposite directions give a standing wave, `y = 2A sin(kx) cos(omega t)`, with nodes that never move. Two waves of slightly different wavelengths give groups of large and small oscillations, the spatial version of beats.

The three graphs share the same vertical scale (in cm), so the values read at the yellow line can be added directly. Play the animation, or step it, to see that the relationship holds at every instant.
