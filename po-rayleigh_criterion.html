Any lens, mirror or pupil is a circular aperture, so it images a point of light not as a point but as an Airy disk, surrounded by faint rings. Two point sources (two stars, two headlights, two dots) at an angle `Delta theta` apart give two Airy patterns on the screen. The two sources are independent (incoherent), so their intensities simply add: there is no interference between them.

When the patterns overlap too much, they merge into one blob and the two sources cannot be told apart. Rayleigh’s criterion: two sources are just resolved when the central peak of one falls on the first dark ring of the other,

`theta_R = 1.22 lambda/D`   (in radians; `D` the diameter of the aperture).

At that separation the combined intensity dips to about 73.5% between the peaks. Closer than `theta_R`, the dip shrinks and disappears near `0.78 theta_R`. For objects a distance `s` apart at a distance `R`, `Delta theta ~~ s/R`, so the farthest distance at which they can be resolved is `R = s/theta_R`.

A larger aperture or a shorter wavelength gives a smaller `theta_R`, so finer detail: this is why telescopes have large mirrors and why blue light resolves better than red. The human eye (pupil about 3 mm) has `theta_R ~~ 2 xx 10^(-4)` rad, about 1 arc-minute. The face-on view uses the same intensities as the graph; the brightening option only makes the faint rings visible.
