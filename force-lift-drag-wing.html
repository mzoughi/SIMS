How the shape of the flow depends on the angle of attack

The cross-section of a wing (an airfoil) is shown in a stream of air, as seen by someone riding with the plane: the plane flies to the left, so the air flows past the wing from left to right at the airspeed `v`. The angle of attack `alpha` is the tilt of the wing into the oncoming air.

The air exerts a force on the wing. Its part across the airflow is the lift `L`; its part along the airflow is the drag `D`. Both have the same form as the drag on a falling ball:

`L = 1/2 rho v^2 S C_L`,   `D = 1/2 rho v^2 S C_D`,

where `rho` is the density of the air, `S` the area of the wing seen from above, and `C_L` and `C_D` the lift and drag coefficients, which depend on the shape of the wing and on the angle of attack. Doubling the speed makes both forces four times larger.

Where lift comes from. The tilted, curved wing turns the air flowing past it downward (the downwash behind the wing). By Newton’s third law, if the wing pushes the air down, the air pushes the wing up. Equivalently, the air flows faster over the top of the wing than under it, so the pressure is lower above than below (Bernoulli), and the pressure difference pushes the wing up. Both descriptions are correct; they are two views of the same flow.

Angle of attack and stall. Tilting the wing more turns more air downward: `C_L` grows almost in proportion to `alpha`. But beyond about 15°, the air can no longer follow the curved top surface: it breaks away into swirls, the lift drops and the drag rises sharply. The wing has stalled. The drag also grows with the lift (turning the air down has a cost): `C_D` is smallest near zero lift and grows like `C_L^2`.

Level flight. The plane flies level when the lift balances its weight, `L = m g`. At a given angle of attack this needs a certain speed, `v = sqrt((2 m g)/(rho S C_L))`. The slowest speed at which it can stay up is the stall speed, at the largest `C_L`. Higher up the air is thinner, so the plane must fly faster for the same lift. (The drag must be balanced by the thrust of the engine, not shown here.)

Model. A light-plane wing (an airfoil like the NACA 2412, aspect ratio 8): `C_L` = 0.11 per degree times (`alpha` + 2°) up to 15°, then falling; `C_D` = 0.008 + 0.05 `C_L^2`, rising sharply after the stall. Air density 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level, decreasing with altitude. The streamlines are drawn to show the main features of the flow (bending over the wing, downwash, separation at the stall); they are not a computed flow.
