Early experiments revealed three types of nuclear radiation, alpha (`alpha`), beta (`beta`) and gamma (`gamma`), told apart by how far they penetrate matter and by how they behave in a magnetic field.

Penetration. A radioactive source sends radiation toward a Geiger–Müller counter; each ionizing particle or photon entering its tube makes it click. Alpha radiation is barely able to pass through a thin sheet of paper (or a few centimetres of air); beta radiation passes paper but penetrates aluminium only to about 3 mm; gamma radiation penetrates lead to 2 cm or more and is only gradually reduced. Because decays are random, the count varies from second to second (typical error `sqrt(N)`); the counter also picks up background radiation, about 0.5 counts per second here. Moving the counter away lowers the rate roughly as `1/d^2`.

Magnetic field. The force on a moving charge is `vec F = q vec v xx vec B`. With `vec B` into the page and the beam moving to the right, positively charged particles are deflected upward, negatively charged ones downward, and uncharged radiation passes undeflected. This identified alpha rays as helium nuclei (charge +2e), beta rays as electrons (and positrons, `+e`), and gamma rays as photons. Since the force is perpendicular to the velocity, each particle moves on a circle of radius `r = p/(|q|B)`. Alpha particles have twice the charge of an electron but a far larger momentum, so they are deflected much less: like Figure 12.5.2, the display can exaggerate their bend (the factor is shown), and the table gives the true radii. Beta particles share their energy with a neutrino, so they emerge with a range of energies and the beta beam fans out.
