If the vibration is violent enough, the nucleus stretches, pinches and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing two or three neutrons and a large amount of energy (Figure 12.6.3).

Where the energy comes from: binding energy per nucleon
Neutrons from one fission can split more U-235 nuclei: a chain reaction (Figure 12.6.4). If the sample is too small, too many neutrons escape through the surface before they cause fission and the chain dies out. The minimum mass for a self-sustaining chain reaction is the critical mass; reaching it is called criticality. U-238, 99.3% of natural uranium, absorbs neutrons without splitting, so natural uranium cannot sustain a chain reaction.

Why size matters

The splitting of a nucleus is called fission. A neutron absorbed by `\ ^235 U` forms `\ ^236 U`, which splits into two smaller nuclei and two or three neutrons, for example `\ _0^1 n + \ _92^235 U -> \ _56^141 Ba + \ _36^92 Kr + 3\ _0^1 n + Q`. The products are lighter than the reactants, so the reaction is exothermic: `Q = (Delta m) c^2 ~~ 200` MeV, carried away mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments. The fragments vary from one fission to the next, with peaks near `A ~~ 95` and `A ~~ 137`.

The energy release can be understood from the binding energy per nucleon: uranium lies below the fragments, which are closer to the iron peak, so the nucleons of the fragments are more tightly bound. In the liquid drop model, the neutron sets the nucleus vibrating; if the vibration is violent enough, it splits.

The neutrons released can cause further fissions: a chain reaction. If the sample is too small, too many neutrons escape through its surface; the minimum mass for a self-sustaining chain reaction is the critical mass. U-238 absorbs neutrons without splitting, so natural uranium (0.7% U-235) cannot sustain a chain reaction; with a moderator that slows the neutrons, uranium enriched to a few percent can.
