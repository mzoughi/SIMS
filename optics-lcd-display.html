How an LCD Screen Works

a backlight, two crossed polarizers, and a liquid crystal that twists the light · a voltage untwists it

A liquid crystal display (LCD) does not make light: it controls how much of the light from a backlight gets through each tiny spot of the screen. Every pixel is made of three subpixels, behind a red, a green and a blue colour filter. Seen from a distance, the three blend: red + green looks yellow, all three look white. Each subpixel works like a light valve controlled by a voltage.

1. The layers, from back to front: the backlight (LEDs and a diffuser); a rear polarizer; a glass plate carrying a grid of tiny transistors (the thin-film transistors, TFT) and a transparent electrode for each subpixel; a thin layer of liquid crystal, a few micrometres thick; the colour filters and a common transparent electrode on the front glass; and a front polarizer, turned 90° from the rear one. Remove any layer to see its role.

2. Polarization. Light is a wave whose electric field oscillates across its direction of travel. The backlight is unpolarized: its field points every which way. A polarizer passes only the part along its axis: unpolarized light loses half its intensity. Polarized light meeting a second polarizer at an angle `theta` to its polarization keeps a fraction given by Malus’s law,

`I = I_0 cos^2 theta`.

Crossed polarizers (`theta` = 90°) block all of it. The liquid crystal molecules are long rods. The glass surfaces are brushed so that the rods line up along the rear polarizer at the back and along the front polarizer at the front; in between they form a gentle 90° twist (a twisted nematic cell). Light passing through follows the twist: its polarization is turned by 90°, so it now lines up with the front polarizer and gets through. The subpixel is bright.

A voltage across the layer (a few volts) makes the rods turn to point along the electric field, toward the viewer. The twist is undone; the light keeps its original polarization and the crossed front polarizer blocks it. The subpixel is dark. In between, partial untwisting gives the shades of gray: if the polarization is turned by `phi`, the transmitted fraction is `sin^2 phi`. This kind of display is “normally white”: bright with no voltage. With parallel polarizers it would be “normally black”.

Model. Threshold voltage 1.5 V; above it the rods tilt and the twist decreases smoothly, nearly fully undone at 5 V. The rotation of the polarization is taken equal to the twist (a simplification). The picture is 32 × 20 pixels; real screens have millions.
