The Magnus Effect: Spin Makes It Curve

An object that spins as it moves through the air is pushed sideways, at right angles to its motion: this is the Magnus effect, named after Heinrich Gustav Magnus, who showed it in 1852 with a spinning brass cylinder in a stream of air (Isaac Newton had already noticed it in 1672, watching tennis players). The push is toward the side where the surface of the object turns with the oncoming air… in other words, toward the side the front of the ball is turning toward.

1. Why it happens. The ball is shown in its own frame: it is still and the air flows past it. A spinning ball drags the air next to it around with it, because of the air’s viscosity and the roughness of the ball. On one side this adds to the speed of the air streaming past; on the other side it takes away from it. Two ways to see the result, both correct: (a) Bernoulli: where the air moves faster, its pressure is lower, so the pressure difference pushes the ball toward the fast side; (b) Newton’s third law: the spinning ball deflects the air behind it to one side (the wake is turned), so the air pushes the ball to the other side. For a ball,

`F_M = 1/2 rho A v^2 C_L`,   `S = (r omega)/v`,

where `S`, the spin parameter, compares the speed of the ball’s surface due to the spin, `r omega`, with the speed of the ball, `v`. The lift coefficient `C_L` grows with `S`; here it is modelled by the simple approximation `C_L ~~ S/(1 + 2S)`. Real values depend on the surface (seams, felt, dimples) and are uncertain; a very smooth ball can even curve the wrong way (the inverse Magnus effect). For a long spinning cylinder in an ideal flow, the force per unit length is `L' = rho v Gamma`, with `Gamma = 2 pi omega r^2`. The streamlines drawn are those of this ideal flow; the swirls show the real wake, turned to one side.

2. Balls in flight. The ball is launched and followed in three dimensions under its weight, the drag of the air and the Magnus force. Backspin (the bottom of the ball moving forward) gives an upward force: the ball stays up longer, as in golf. Topspin gives a downward force: the ball dips, as in tennis, table tennis and a baseball curveball. Sidespin (spin about a vertical axis) makes the ball curve to the side: a soccer “banana kick”, a golf slice or hook. The faint path is the same ball without spin.

3. Rotor ship. A rotor ship (like the E-Ship 1) has tall spinning cylinders, Flettner rotors, instead of sails. When the wind blows across the ship, the spinning rotors are pushed at right angles to the wind, and much of this push is forward. Like a sailing ship, it needs wind; the spin direction must be chosen to push forward. The wind felt on board, the apparent wind, combines the true wind and the wind made by the ship’s own motion. Model: four rotors 27 m tall and 4 m wide; `C_L` up to about 10 for a fast-spinning rotor; the water’s resistance on the hull grows as the square of the ship’s speed; the sideways push is assumed to be resisted by the hull.

4. Rotor airplane. Some airplanes (Flettner airplanes) were built with spinning cylinders instead of wings. A fast-spinning rotor gives much more lift for its size than a wing, so the plane can fly slower, but an engine must keep the rotors spinning, and their drag is large. Model: two rotors, 4.5 m long and 0.8 m wide; compared with a 16 m² light-plane wing.
