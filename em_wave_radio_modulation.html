How Radio Waves Carry Sound: AM and FM

What is happening

Sound frequencies (20 Hz to 20 kHz) are far too low to be sent efficiently as electromagnetic waves: an antenna must be comparable to the wavelength, and `lambda = c//f` is 15 km at 20 kHz. A radio station therefore sends a high-frequency carrier wave and modulates it with the sound signal (the message).

AM (540–1700 kHz) changes the amplitude of the carrier: for a single tone of frequency `f_m`, `s(t) = A_c [1 + mu cos(2 pi f_m t)] cos(2 pi f_c t)`, where `mu` is the modulation index. The outline of the wave (the envelope) has the shape of the sound. With `mu > 1` the envelope folds over and the sound is distorted (overmodulation). Using `cos a cos b = 1/2 [cos(a - b) + cos(a + b)]`,

`s(t) = A_c cos(2 pi f_c t) + (mu A_c)/2 cos(2 pi (f_c - f_m) t) + (mu A_c)/2 cos(2 pi (f_c + f_m) t)`:

the AM wave is exactly the sum of three waves, the carrier and two side frequencies `f_c +- f_m`. They beat with each other, and the beats are the envelope. The signal occupies a bandwidth `2 f_m`, which is why AM stations are 10 kHz apart.

FM (88–108 MHz) keeps the amplitude constant and changes the frequency: `s(t) = A_c cos(2 pi f_c t + beta sin(2 pi f_m t))`, with peak frequency deviation `Delta f` and modulation index `beta = Delta f//f_m`. It also contains side frequencies `f_c +- n f_m` with amplitudes given by Bessel functions `J_n(beta)`; most of the power lies within the Carson bandwidth `2(Delta f + f_m)`. Noise mostly changes the amplitude, which FM ignores, so FM sounds cleaner.

The graphs are not to scale in time: a real carrier makes hundreds or thousands of cycles for each cycle of the sound. The drawing slider sets how many are drawn; the readouts give the real values.
