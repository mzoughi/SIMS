How an LCD Screen Works

A liquid crystal display (LCD) does not make light: it controls how much of the light from a backlight gets through each tiny spot of the screen. Every pixel is made of three subpixels, behind a red, a green and a blue colour filter. Seen from a distance, the three blend: red + green looks yellow, all three look white. Each subpixel is a light valve controlled by a voltage.

1. The layers, numbered in the same way in all tabs, from back to front: ① the backlight; ② a rear polarizer; ③ a glass plate carrying, for every subpixel, a tiny transistor (a thin-film transistor, TFT) and a transparent pixel electrode; ④ a thin layer of liquid crystal, a few micrometres thick; ⑤ the front glass, with the colour filters and a common electrode shared by all subpixels; ⑥ a front polarizer, turned 90° from the rear one. The image signal enters at layer ③: driver chips on the edge of the glass turn the numbers sent by the computer into a voltage on each pixel electrode. That voltage, across the liquid crystal, decides how much light the subpixel lets through.

2. Building the picture. The computer sends the picture as numbers: three numbers from 0 to 255 (red, green, blue) for every pixel. The pixels are wired in a grid. A row driver switches on the transistors of one row at a time (the gate line); at the same moment a column driver puts the right voltage on every column (the data lines), and the pixel electrodes of that row charge to those voltages. Then the next row, and so on, from top to bottom: one frame. A small capacitor at each subpixel holds its voltage until the same row is written again in the next frame. A 1920 × 1080 screen writes 1080 rows 60 times a second: each row gets about 15 µs. The SIM slows this down enormously; without the holding capacitor, each row would fade before it is written again, and the screen would flicker.

3. Polarization. Light is a wave whose electric field oscillates across its direction of travel. The backlight is unpolarized: its field points every which way. A polarizer passes only the part along its axis: unpolarized light loses half its intensity. Polarized light meeting a second polarizer at an angle `theta` to its polarization keeps the fraction given by Malus’s law,

`I = I_0 cos^2 theta`.

Crossed polarizers (`theta` = 90°) block all of it. The liquid crystal molecules are long rods. The glass surfaces are treated so that the rods line up along the rear polarizer at the back and along the front polarizer at the front; in between they form a gentle 90° twist. Light passing through follows the twist: its polarization is turned by 90°, it lines up with the front polarizer and passes. A voltage across the layer (a few volts) makes the rods turn to point along the electric field; the twist is undone, the polarization is not turned, and the crossed front polarizer blocks the light. In between, partial untwisting gives the shades: if the polarization is turned by `phi`, the fraction passed is `sin^2 phi`. The colour filter then lets through only its own colour. This kind of display is “normally white”: bright with no voltage. A white pixel (255) needs 0 V; a black one (0) needs about 5 V.

Model. Threshold voltage 1.5 V; above it the rods tilt and the twist decreases smoothly, nearly fully undone at 5 V. The rotation of the polarization is taken equal to the twist, and the image value is taken proportional to the brightness (real screens add a correction called gamma). The picture is 32 × 20 pixels; real screens have millions.
